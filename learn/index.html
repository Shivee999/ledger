Lessons you can poke at.

Five interactive lessons in computer science and commerce. Each one shows its working rather than just an answer, so a student can reproduce it by hand — which is what an exam actually asks for. Everything runs in the browser; nothing is uploaded, nothing is tracked.

Computer science · Number systems

Binary, decimal, hex and octal

Convert between all four bases at once — and see the division-and-remainder method written out underneath, step by step, so the answer can be checked by hand.

Class 9–12 computer science, undergraduate CS foundations
Computer science · Digital electronics

Logic gates and truth tables

Flip two switches and watch seven gates respond live. The truth table below highlights the row matching your current inputs, connecting the abstract table to something moving.

Digital electronics, computer organisation
Computer science · Algorithms

Sorting algorithms, counted

Bubble, selection, insertion and merge sort running on the same data, with live comparison and swap counters. The counters are the lesson: they show why two algorithms that both work are not equally good.

Data structures and algorithms, class 11–12 and undergraduate
Computer science · Computer organisation

How a CPU runs an instruction

Step through fetch, decode and execute one stage at a time, watching the program counter, registers and memory change as a tiny program adds two numbers.

Computer organisation and architecture
Commerce · Business studies

Break-even and unit economics

Contribution per unit, break-even point, P/V ratio and margin of safety — each with the formula written out in full. Change any input and every figure recalculates.

Class 11–12 commerce and accountancy, business studies, BBA

For teachers

These are free to use in class, project on a board, or send to students — no attribution needed, no account required. Each page is a single self-contained file, so once it has loaded it keeps working without internet, which matters in a lab with unreliable wifi.

They are deliberately narrow. Each does one concept properly rather than covering a whole syllabus, and each states its own assumptions and limits where they exist. If a lesson would help your subject and isn't here, it probably just hasn't been built yet.
